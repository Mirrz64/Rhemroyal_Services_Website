"How do you measure your impact?" is one of the most common questions small community organisations get asked — by funders, by partners, sometimes just by their own volunteers. It's also one of the questions people put off answering, because it sounds like it requires a research department they don't have.

It doesn't. It requires a simple framework, applied consistently. Here's where to start.

## Start with the change you're trying to create

Before measuring anything, write one sentence describing the change your work creates, for whom. Not the activity — the change. "We run a weekly food programme" is an activity. "Families in our area have reliable access to food during the school holidays" is a change. Measurement gets easier once you're clear which one you're actually tracking.

## Separate outputs from outcomes

This is the single most useful distinction in impact measurement.

- **Outputs** are what you did: meals served, sessions run, people attended.
- **Outcomes** are what changed as a result: confidence improved, food insecurity reduced, skills gained.

Outputs are easy to count and easy to over-rely on. Funders and partners increasingly want to see outcomes too — evidence that the activity actually changed something, not just that it happened.

## Pick two or three outcome measures, not twenty

Small teams often try to measure everything and end up measuring nothing consistently. Choose two or three outcomes that genuinely matter to your mission, and track those properly — a short survey, a simple before/after question, a consistent case note — rather than spreading effort across measures no one has time to maintain.

## Build measurement into delivery, not on top of it

The measurement that survives is the measurement that takes almost no extra effort to collect. If it requires a separate process bolted on after the fact, it tends to quietly stop happening within a few months. Ask one short question as part of something you're already doing, rather than adding a whole new step.

> You don't need perfect data to start. You need consistent, honest data collected the same way each time.

## Turn it into a story you can tell

Numbers alone rarely move funders or partners. The combination of a clear number and one honest example — a specific change in a specific person's circumstances — is usually what makes an impact report memorable rather than just accurate.
